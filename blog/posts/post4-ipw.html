1. From identification to estimation

Post 2 ended with the identification formula on the hazard scale:

S^a(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k} \left(1 - \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a)\right)\right].

Post 3 estimated this by plug-in: fit the conditional hazard \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a), average per-subject products. The g-formula models the outcome process.

IPW takes a different factoring. By a Horvitz-Thompson identity, under the same three identifiability conditions of post 2 §3,

S^a(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\mathbb{1}\{A = a\}}{g(a \mid \mathbf L)}\, \mathbb{1}\{Y_k = 0\}\right].

Derivation: from S^a(k) to the Horvitz-Thompson identity

Claim. Under consistency, exchangeability, and positivity,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\mathbb{1}\{A=a\}}{g(a \mid \mathbf L)}\, \mathbb{1}\{Y_k = 0\}\right] \;=\; S^a(k).

Proof.

Step 1 (algebra, law of total expectation): \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\frac{1}{g(a \mid \mathbf L)}\, \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}\{A=a\}\,\mathbb{1}\{Y_k = 0\} \mid \mathbf L]\right].

Step 2 (consistency): when A = a, Y_k = Y^a_k. So \mathbb{1}\{A=a\}\,\mathbb{1}\{Y_k=0\} = \mathbb{1}\{A=a\}\,\mathbb{1}\{Y^a_k=0\}.

Step 3 (exchangeability Y^a_k \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid \mathbf L): the joint expectation factors. First, the conditional expectation of a product of indicators is a joint probability: \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}\{A=a\}\,\mathbb{1}\{Y^a_k=0\} \mid \mathbf L] = \mathbb{P}[A=a,\ Y^a_k=0 \mid \mathbf L].

An indicator equals 1 when its event occurs and 0 otherwise, so its expectation is the probability of that event. A product of two indicators equals 1 only when both events occur, so its expectation is the joint probability of both. No assumption is used here, only the definition of an indicator.

Exchangeability says that, given \mathbf L, the counterfactual outcome Y^a_k carries no information about which treatment was actually received. Independence means the joint probability splits into the product of the two marginals: \mathbb{P}[A=a,\ Y^a_k=0 \mid \mathbf L] = \mathbb{P}[A=a \mid \mathbf L]\;\mathbb{P}[Y^a_k=0 \mid \mathbf L].

This is the step that would fail without no-unmeasured-confounding: if some confounder U were left out of \mathbf L, then A and Y^a_k would still be linked through U given \mathbf L, and the joint would not split.

The first factor is the propensity by definition, \mathbb{P}[A=a \mid \mathbf L] = g(a \mid \mathbf L). Substituting: \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}\{A=a\}\,\mathbb{1}\{Y^a_k=0\} \mid \mathbf L] = g(a \mid \mathbf L)\, \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0 \mid \mathbf L].

This is the only step that uses the no-unmeasured-confounding assumption: without it, the joint does not factor, and the propensity in the denominator would not cancel the g(a \mid \mathbf L) produced here.

Step 4 (positivity g(a \mid \mathbf L) > 0): cancel and average. Substitute the result of Step 3 back into the Step 1 expression. The inverse propensity out front meets the propensity just produced: \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\frac{1}{g(a \mid \mathbf L)}\, g(a \mid \mathbf L)\, \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0 \mid \mathbf L]\right].

The cancellation \tfrac{1}{g(a \mid \mathbf L)}\,g(a \mid \mathbf L) = 1 is only legal when g(a \mid \mathbf L) > 0, i.e. every covariate stratum has some chance of receiving treatment a. This is exactly positivity. What remains is \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0 \mid \mathbf L]\right].

Finally, averaging a conditional probability over the distribution of \mathbf L returns the marginal probability (law of total expectation), and the marginal is the counterfactual survival by definition: \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0 \mid \mathbf L]\right] = \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0] = S^a(k). \qquad \blacksquare

Step Assumption Role
2 Consistency Replace observed Y_k by counterfactual Y^a_k
3 Exchangeability Factor the joint to extract g(a \mid \mathbf L)
4 Positivity Cancel g(a \mid \mathbf L) in numerator and denominator

The same three assumptions as g-formula identification, factored differently.

The right-hand side is the expected survival in a reweighted population where each subject is up-weighted by the inverse of their probability of having received the treatment they did. The weight w_i = 1/g(A_i \mid \mathbf L_i) creates a pseudo-population in which baseline covariates \mathbf L are independent of treatment A.

What is a pseudo-population?

A reweighted version of the original sample. Original rows with a weight column attached.

When we say “pseudo-population of size \sum_i w_i,” we treat each subject i as contributing w_i virtual copies.

Why A \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf L in the pseudo-population. The original joint density factors as

\mathbb{P}[A=a,\, \mathbf L = \mathbf l] = g(a \mid \mathbf l)\, f(\mathbf l).

In the pseudo-population, each (A, \mathbf L) cell is weighted by 1/g(A \mid \mathbf L).

The plug-in idea is to replace each unknown ingredient:

  • Replace g(A_i \mid \mathbf L_i) with a fitted propensity \hat g(A_i \mid \mathbf L_i), modelled via logistic regression on baseline data.
  • Replace \mathbb{E} with the weighted empirical estimator over the observed sample.

The resulting IPW estimator is the weighted Kaplan-Meier applied within each arm (§4):

\hat S^a_{\text{IPW}}(k) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{k}\big(1 - \hat\lambda^{\text{KM},a}_j\big),

with weighted hazards constructed from the pseudo-population. The contrast follows: \hat\Delta(k) = \hat S^1_{\text{IPW}}(k) - \hat S^0_{\text{IPW}}(k).

2. The propensity model

The propensity score g(A \mid \mathbf L) is the conditional probability of receiving treatment A given baseline covariates \mathbf L. In the binary-treatment setting, it is estimated by logistic regression on the subject-level data:

\text{logit}\, \hat g(A = 1 \mid \mathbf L) \;=\; \alpha_0 + \boldsymbol\alpha_L^{\!\top} \mathbf L.

The model is fit by maximum likelihood using each subject’s baseline row only (one row per subject). The fitted output is a probability in [0, 1] for every subject: \hat g(A_i = 1 \mid \mathbf L_i) for treated, 1 - \hat g(A_i = 1 \mid \mathbf L_i) for untreated. Section 3 inverts these to build the weights.

Variable inclusion. The model should include all measured confounders, the variables that motivate the analysis (post 2 §3).1

3. Building the weights

For each subject i, the unstabilized weight is the inverse of the fitted propensity at their observed treatment:

w_i \;=\; \frac{1}{\hat g(A_i \mid \mathbf L_i)}.

This weight creates a pseudo-population in which A \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf L (§1 collapsible). In practice the stabilized weights are preferred due to lower estimator variance (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000), where the weight replaces the unit numerator with the marginal probability of receiving A_i:

SW^A_i \;=\; \frac{\mathbb{P}[A = A_i]}{\hat g(A_i \mid \mathbf L_i)}.

Stabilized weights are the default in the package.2

Weight diagnostics. Before using the weights, check the distribution: mean ≈ 1 for stabilized weights under correctly specified models, with a tight range and no extreme outliers. Extreme weights signal either positivity violations (some covariate-treatment cells have very small propensity) or misspecification of the propensity model. Weight diagnostics are the first sanity check on every IPW analysis.3

4. The IPW algorithm: weighted Kaplan-Meier

After §3, every subject has a stabilized weight SW^A_i. The IPW estimator computes the weighted Kaplan-Meier curve within each arm.

1. Apply weights to person-time. Each subject’s stabilized weight SW^A_i is constant across all their at-risk intervals (treatment is baseline-only, so the weight is too). Attach SW^A_i as a row-level column to the person-time dataset of post 1 §5. Every row of subject i carries the same weight.

2. Compute the weighted hazard in each arm. At each interval j, within each arm a, the weighted hazard is

\hat\lambda^{\text{KM},a}_j \;=\; \frac{\sum_i SW^A_i\, \mathbb{1}\{Y_{i,j}=1,\, Y_{i,j-1}=0,\, A_i=a\}} {\sum_i SW^A_i\, \mathbb{1}\{Y_{i,j-1}=0,\, \bar C_{i,j-1}=0,\, A_i=a\}}.

The numerator is the weighted count of events at interval j in arm a. The denominator is the weighted count of at-risk subjects entering interval j in the same arm. The standard Kaplan-Meier hazard formula from post 1 §3, with sums replaced by weighted sums.

3. Cumulative product. The arm-specific weighted survival is

\hat S^a_{\text{IPW}}(k) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{k}\big(1 - \hat\lambda^{\text{KM},a}_j\big).

The contrast follows:

\hat\Delta(k) \;=\; \hat S^1_{\text{IPW}}(k) - \hat S^0_{\text{IPW}}(k).

5. An alternative: the marginal structural model (MSM)

The weighted Kaplan-Meier of §4 is nonparametric in time: the hazard at each interval j is a separate quantity with its own weighted-empirical estimator. No smoothness across intervals is assumed.

The marginal structural model (Robins, 1999) imposes a parametric form on the time function instead. The MSM models the counterfactual hazard as

\text{logit}\, \lambda^{Y, \text{MSM}}_j(a) \;=\; \beta_0(j) + \beta_A\, a,

where \beta_0(j) is a smooth function of the interval index (splines, polynomials, or interval indicators) and \beta_A is the log odds ratio of the discrete-time hazard. Writing the hazard odds at interval j under treatment a as \lambda^{Y,\text{MSM}}_j(a) / \big(1 - \lambda^{Y,\text{MSM}}_j(a)\big), the model says

e^{\beta_A} \;=\; \frac{\lambda^{Y,\text{MSM}}_j(1)\big/\big(1 - \lambda^{Y,\text{MSM}}_j(1)\big)}{\lambda^{Y,\text{MSM}}_j(0)\big/\big(1 - \lambda^{Y,\text{MSM}}_j(0)\big)},

the ratio of the hazard odds under treatment (a=1) to the hazard odds under control (a=0). The baseline \beta_0(j) cancels, so this odds ratio is assumed constant across intervals j. It approximates the hazard ratio only when the per-interval hazard is small (short intervals, rare events), where the odds and the probability nearly coincide. The model is fit on the person-time dataset by weighted pooled-logistic regression, with weights SW^A_i from §3.

The weighted likelihood

Pooled-logistic regression treats each at-risk person-interval row (i,j) as a Bernoulli trial with success probability equal to the hazard \lambda_j(a_i) = \text{expit}\big(\beta_0(j) + \beta_A a_i\big). The contribution of a single row to the log-likelihood is

\ell_{i,j}(\beta) \;=\; Y_{i,j}\,\log \lambda_j(a_i) \;+\; (1 - Y_{i,j})\,\log\big(1 - \lambda_j(a_i)\big).

The unweighted estimator maximizes \sum_i \sum_{j \le K_i} \ell_{i,j}(\beta) over all at-risk rows. The MSM instead maximizes the weighted log-likelihood, with each row scaled by the subject’s stabilized weight,

\ell_W(\beta) \;=\; \sum_i \sum_{j \le K_i} SW^A_i \,\ell_{i,j}(\beta),

equivalently solving the weighted score equations \sum_i \sum_{j \le K_i} SW^A_i\,\big(Y_{i,j} - \lambda_j(a_i)\big)\,x_{i,j} = 0, where x_{i,j} = \big(\text{basis}(j),\, a_i\big). Because SW^A_i is constant across a subject’s rows (treatment is baseline-only), a subject with weight SW^A_i = 2 contributes as two identical copies; reweighting the sample in this way recreates the pseudo-population in which A \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf L, which is what licenses omitting \mathbf L from the linear predictor while still reading \hat\beta_A as causal. In software this is glm(..., family = binomial, weights = SW).

One consequence of weighting: the rows of a given subject are correlated, so the model-based (naive) standard errors from the weighted fit are anti-conservative. Valid inference uses a robust sandwich variance or the nonparametric bootstrap (resampling subjects, refitting the propensity and the MSM at each replicate). The point estimate \hat\beta_A is unaffected; only its standard error requires the correction.

Two terms in the name deserve unpacking.

Marginal. The right-hand side has no \mathbf L. Covariate information is absorbed by the weights. In the pseudo-population of §1, A \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf L, so conditioning on \mathbf L or not does not change the relationship between A and Y in the pseudo-population. The MSM marginalizes via the weights before fitting.

Structural. The fitted hazard targets the counterfactual hazard \lambda^{Y^a}_j, not an observational conditional. The parameter \beta_A is a property of the counterfactual data-generating process, not of the observed data alone.

The arm-specific survival curve follows from the product representation:

\hat S^a_{\text{IPW-MSM}}(k) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{k} \big(1 - \hat\lambda^{Y, \text{MSM}}_j(a)\big).

The same target as the weighted KM of §4; the only difference is that \beta_0(j) smooths the hazard across intervals. With a flexible spline on j, the MSM curve closely resembles the weighted KM step function, smoother but tracking the same shape. With a restrictive form (no time effect, say), the MSM imposes additional structure that may not be satisfied.4

Comparing model requirements: g-formula vs IPW

The g-formula and IPW share the same identification formula and target the same S^a(k) but differ in which models they fit and what the analyst must specify.

g-formula IPW
Model 1 Y-hazard \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A) Propensity \hat g(A \mid \mathbf L)
Model 2 — Y-hazard or MSM on the pseudo-population
Marginalization Empirical (1/n)\sum_i over \mathbf L_i, after fitting Absorbed into weights, before fitting
Censoring weight Implicit (uncensored at-risk rows) Explicit C-hazard model (§7)
Fourth assumption Y-hazard correctly specified Propensity correctly specified

The g-formula models the outcome process and standardizes over the empirical covariate distribution. The IPW estimator models the treatment process and uses the weights to do the marginalization automatically; an additional model (MSM) is fit on the pseudo-population for §5’s parametric route.

When the Y-hazard model is correctly specified but the propensity is hard to model, the g-formula has the advantage. When the propensity is well-understood but the outcome is hard to model, IPW has the advantage. Doubly robust estimators combine both and require only one of the two to be correctly specified.

6. Worked example

We run the IPW algorithm on the simulated observational DGP from post 2: 1000 patients, age and sex measured at baseline, drug assignment depending on both. The true treatment effect is known from the DGP, so estimates can be compared.

Three estimates of arm-specific survival are computed:

  • Naive arm-specific Kaplan-Meier. No weighting, no adjustment. Computed by calling the package’s KM helper weighted_hazard_by_k() with weights of 1.
  • IPW weighted Kaplan-Meier. Propensity logistic fit on baseline, stabilized weights constructed, weighted KM applied per arm (the §4 algorithm) via causal_survival(method = "ipw_km").
  • DGP truth. Arm-specific counterfactual survival computed analytically from the simulation script, what \hat S^a_{\text{IPW}} should recover under correct specification and the identifiability conditions of post 2 §3 (plus the weight model correctness from §3).

The naive arm-specific KM is biased because the treated and untreated groups differ in age and sex. The crude curves estimate the observational \mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0 \mid A = a], not the counterfactual S^a(k).

The IPW weighted KM curves recover the truth (up to sampling variation) because the propensity model adjusts for \mathbf L = (\text{age}, \text{sex}), the only confounders in our DGP. The four assumptions are satisfied here by construction: exchangeability given \mathbf L, positivity, consistency, and correct specification of the propensity model.

At month 48 (end of follow-up), the estimates are:

Quantity Naive KM IPW Truth
S(48 \mid A=1) TBD TBD TBD
S(48 \mid A=0) TBD TBD TBD
\Delta(48) TBD TBD TBD

The IPW estimator recovers the causal risk difference within sampling variation;5 the naive KM does not.

7. Censoring under IPW: IPCW

The IPCW estimator requires three conditions in addition to those of §3:

  1. Conditional exchangeability of censoring given (\mathbf L, A).
  2. Positivity of censoring, every covariate-treatment profile has some chance of remaining uncensored at each interval.
  3. Correct specification of the censoring-hazard model.

Failure of any of them biases the estimator, parallel to the treatment-side conditions. The rest of this section describes the procedure.

Post 3 §5 handled censoring under the g-formula implicitly: the Y-hazard model was fit on uncensored at-risk rows, and the estimator targeted the no-censoring counterfactual under conditional exchangeability of censoring given (\mathbf L, A). IPW handles censoring explicitly. The censoring process gets its own model and its own weights, in parallel to the treatment weighting of §2–§3.

The censoring-hazard model. Pooled-logistic regression for the conditional censoring probability at each interval:

\text{logit}\, \hat\lambda^C_j(\mathbf L, A) \;=\; \gamma_0(j) + \gamma_A\, A + \boldsymbol\gamma_L^{\!\top} \mathbf L,

fit on the person-time dataset with C_{i,j} as outcome among at-risk uncensored rows. The time function \gamma_0(j) follows the same conventions as the Y-hazard model.

The censoring weight. Each subject accumulates an inverse-probability-of-being-uncensored weight across intervals:

w^C_i(j) \;=\; \prod_{m=1}^{j} \frac{1}{1 - \hat\lambda^C_m(\mathbf L_i, A_i)}.

By interval j, subject i has avoided censoring j times. Each step contributes its own inverse-of-uncensored probability to the cumulative weight. An uncensored subject whose covariate profile makes early censoring likely gets a large weight at later intervals, they “stand in for” patients who were censored but had similar covariates.

The combined weight. Treatment and censoring weights multiply:

W_i(j) \;=\; SW^A_i \times w^C_i(j).

The treatment weight breaks A \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf L in the pseudo-population; the censoring weight breaks C_j \perp\!\!\!\perp (\mathbf L, A). The combined weight does both.

Why does multiplicativity follow from the joint factorization?

Each weight has a single job. SW^A_i breaks the association between A and \mathbf L. w^C_i(j) breaks the association between C_j and (\mathbf L, A). To obtain both, we need a weight that does both jobs in the same pseudo-population.

The joint density of treatment and uncensored survival to interval j factors as

\mathbb{P}[A=a,\ \bar C_j = 0,\ \mathbf L = \mathbf l] \;=\; f(\mathbf l) \cdot g(a \mid \mathbf l) \cdot \prod_{m=1}^{j} \mathbb{P}[C_m = 0 \mid \bar C_{m-1}=0,\, \mathbf L,\, A].

Inverting each factor and multiplying gives:

W_i(j) \;=\; \frac{1}{g(A_i \mid \mathbf L_i)} \times \prod_{m=1}^{j} \frac{1}{\mathbb{P}[C_m = 0 \mid \ldots,\, \mathbf L_i,\, A_i]} \;=\; SW^A_i \times w^C_i(j).

The §4 weighted KM and §5 MSM both run on the combined stabilized weights W_i(j) rather than on SW^A_i. The package toggles this via ipcw = TRUE in causal_survival().

8. Closing

Inverse-probability weighting is the plug-in estimator of the Horvitz-Thompson identification formula of §1. It models the treatment assignment process, the propensity g(A \mid \mathbf L), and uses the inverse propensity as a weight to construct a pseudo-population in which A is independent of \mathbf L. The Kaplan-Meier estimator on the pseudo-population (§4), or a weighted marginal structural model (§5), recovers the counterfactual survival S^a(k) and the contrast \Delta(k).

IPW and the g-formula are two routes to the same target. They differ in which model must be correct: the g-formula relies on the outcome model, IPW on the treatment model. The doubly robust family of estimators combines both and requires only one of the two to be correctly specified.

Pure predictors of the outcome that are not strict confounders can also be included: Brookhart et al. (2006) showed this reduces the variance of the IPW estimator without introducing bias, and Williamson, Forbes & White (2013) extended the result to RCTs. Pure predictors of treatment with no relationship to the outcome should generally be excluded: they inflate weight variance without removing any bias.↩︎

  2. Truncating extreme weights at a percentile cap (e.g., the 99th percentile) reduces estimator variance but introduces bias toward the unadjusted KM, because the pseudo-population cancellation that breaks confounding requires the weight to equal 1/g exactly. Cole & Hernán (2008) recommend truncation as a sensitivity check, not a default fix.↩︎

  3. A thorough treatment of weight diagnostics, the effective sample size n_{\text{eff}} = (\sum w_i)^2 / \sum w_i^2, response options when positivity is borderline (restrict the target population, change the estimand, or match instead of weight), and the distinction between theoretical positivity violations and near-violations in finite samples, is given in Petersen et al. (2012). The present post treats weight diagnostics as a sanity check; full sensitivity analysis is a separate topic.↩︎

  4. A note on the hazard ratio \exp(\hat\beta_A). Three distinct problems make it a poor causal contrast for survival outcomes:

    1. Interpretation. A hazard ratio is a rate ratio, not a duration. A HR of 1.2 does not translate cleanly to “X more months of survival”, the absolute survival difference depends on the baseline hazard, which varies. Two studies reporting the same HR can have wildly different clinical meaning.

    2. Selection on survival. Even under randomization (no confounding at all), the marginal HR varies with t because the at-risk population at t is composed of survivors. High-risk subjects fail earlier, so the at-risk pool at later times is a selected (lower-risk) subset of the originally randomized population. The HR at t = 2 averages over a different population than the HR at t = 1 (Hernán, Ch 17). The IPW-MSM removes confounding bias but not this selection-on-survival mechanism.

    3. Non-collapsibility. The HR is non-collapsible: adding a covariate to the model changes the HR estimate, even when the covariate is unrelated to treatment and not a confounder (Greenland, Robins & Pearl, 1999). The marginal HR is not a weighted average of stratum-specific HRs.

    The counterfactual risk difference \hat\Delta(k) = \hat S^1(k) - \hat S^0(k) avoids all three. The HR interpretation is addressed in a dedicated post.↩︎

  5. Confidence intervals are obtained by non-parametric percentile bootstrap on the subject-level sample. Each bootstrap iteration resamples n subjects with replacement (carrying all their person-time rows) and re-runs the entire pipeline, propensity fit, weights, weighted KM. Per Austin (2016) and Williamson (2013), the robust sandwich variance from weighted regression underestimates the true variance because it treats the estimated weights as fixed; bootstrap captures the full uncertainty.↩︎
