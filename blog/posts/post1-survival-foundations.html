1. The setup: a randomized trial

A 4-year randomized trial enrols 1000 patients at elevated heart-failure risk. Each is assigned, by coin flip, to weekly Drug A or weekly Drug B. The question is which drug delays the onset of heart failure longest.1

Each subject i has a treatment indicator A_i assigned by randomization: A_i = 1 for Drug A, A_i = 0 for Drug B.

By month 48, some patients have had a heart-failure event. Of those without one, some are still event-free when the trial closes — administrative censoring. Others are lost from observation before then — dropout. Either way, the trial cannot say what would eventually have happened to them. Their observations are censored, where censoring is restricted to avoidable causes of incomplete observation.2

The natural-sounding question — “what proportion of patients on Drug A had a heart failure?” — is ill-posed. The denominator is not the same for everyone. A patient followed for 6 months cannot contribute the same information as one followed for 48. Crude proportions throw away the time at risk.

2. Why crude rates fail

In the 1000-patient trial, follow-up ends at month 48. By that point, 312 patients have had a heart-failure event at known times; the remaining 688 have not — 540 are still under observation when the trial closes (administrative censoring), 148 are lost from observation earlier (dropout, also called loss to follow-up). Both censoring types are taken to be independent of the event time.3

The natural first instinct is to summarise this cohort with an average time to event. Two attempts fail.

The average of all 1000 observed times treats each censoring time as if it were an event time. But the 688 censored patients had not yet had an event when their observation ended — their true event times are later than what was recorded. The average pulls downward.

The average of only the 312 uncensored times drops the censored patients. But those patients had survived past their censoring time event-free, and so represent longer-than-average survival that the average no longer sees. The average pulls downward again.

Both summaries underestimate the true mean for the same reason: a censoring time carries partial information — the patient was event-free up to that time — that a naive average cannot exploit (Ravn & Andersen, Exercise 1.1). The same logic extends to other simple summaries — the proportion of events by a fixed month, for example — that ignore the partial information in censored observations.

3. The Kaplan-Meier estimator

Time is divided into discrete intervals — months — and observation proceeds interval by interval. Within each interval, at most one thing happens to a subject: either they have the event (Y = 1), or they are censored (C = 1), or neither. Events and censorings do not coincide in the same interval, so there is no ambiguity about who is at risk during it.

The Kaplan-Meier estimator uses the censored observations correctly. At each interval, it computes the empirical hazard — the fraction of patients at risk who had an event during the interval. The Kaplan-Meier survival estimate at interval k is the product of one-minus-hazard across all earlier intervals:

\hat S_{\text{KM}}(k) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{k} \left(1 - \hat\lambda^Y_j\right),

where the empirical hazard at interval j is

\hat\lambda^Y_j \;=\; \frac{\text{events in interval } j}{\text{patients at risk in interval } j} \;=\; \frac{\sum_i \mathbb{1}\{Y_{i,j}=1,\ Y_{i,j-1}=0\}}{\sum_i \mathbb{1}\{Y_{i,j-1}=0,\ \bar C_{i,j-1}=0\}}.

The product updates only when events occur. The curve is a step function that drops at every observed event and stays flat between. Under independent censoring, \hat S_{\text{KM}}(k) consistently estimates the survival function S(k).

Section 2’s 688 censored patients enter this estimate without being dropped or mislabeled. Each contributes to the denominator of \hat\lambda^Y_j for every interval they were under observation, and exits without entering any numerator. The partial information — that they were event-free up to a certain interval — is fully used; the naive averages of Section 2 could not exploit it.

4. Hazards: the most important tool in survival analysis

Section 3 computed the empirical hazard \hat\lambda^Y_j from a ratio of events to patients at risk. Its population counterpart is the hazard at interval k:

\lambda^Y_k \;=\; \mathbb{P}\!\left[Y_k = 1 \,\middle|\, Y_{k-1} = 0,\ \bar C_{k-1} = 0\right],

the probability of an event during interval k given the patient was at risk at its start (event-free and uncensored through the end of interval k-1). The empirical hazard \hat\lambda^Y_j of Section 3 is one nonparametric estimator of \lambda^Y_j.

A patient is event-free at interval k only by being event-free at every preceding interval. The survival function factorises into one-step survivals:

S(k) \;=\; \mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0] \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1 - \lambda^Y_j\right).

Each factor (1 - \lambda^Y_j) is the per-interval survival probability; their product is the survival probability through interval k.

The product is the structural backbone of the estimators in this series. Kaplan-Meier plugs in the nonparametric empirical hazard. The estimators in the next posts — IPW and the g-formula — plug in modelled \hat\lambda^Y_j that depend on treatment and covariates. The combining machinery stays the same.

5. Person-time data layout

Up to this section, the data have been thought of in wide format: one row per subject, with their observed time, event indicator, and treatment. To compute the empirical hazard \hat\lambda^Y_j at each interval — and, in later posts, to model it as a function of covariates — the data are reshaped into long format, also called person-time format. Each row corresponds to one subject in one at-risk interval.

A subject contributes one row for each interval they are at risk, until they either have the event or are censored. Each row carries the subject’s id i, the interval k, the treatment A_i, the event status at the end of k (Y_{i,k}), and the censoring status during k (C_{i,k}).

In long format, the empirical hazard at interval j is the fraction of rows at interval j with Y_{i,j}=1. The reshape has done the bookkeeping the empirical hazard requires.

Long format is also the substrate for the modelled hazards in the next posts: pooled-logistic regression treats each at-risk row as an observation, with Y_{i,k} as the binary outcome and treatment, covariates, and interval as predictors. The layout introduced here is the input to IPW and the g-formula in posts 3 and 4.

6. Closing

So — does the KM give us everything we need to compute survival contrasts in studies? Under randomization in a perfectly conducted RCT: yes, the arm-specific S(k \mid A=a) and the contrast \Delta(k) are unbiased estimates of the corresponding causal quantities.

These posts focus on observational data, which is harder for multiple reasons — one of the most important being confounding. In observational settings, KM is no longer an unbiased estimate of the causal quantity; the treated and untreated groups differ in ways that affect the outcome independently of the drug. The estimand must be defined more carefully — separating what we would see (the causal survival curve S^a(k)) from what we do see (the observational S(k \mid A=a)) — and the estimator must do additional work to bridge the gap.

The next post introduces the causal estimand S^a(k), the identifiability conditions that link it to the observational data, and the curse of dimensionality that motivates modelling. After that: IPW and the g-formula as two estimation routes.
