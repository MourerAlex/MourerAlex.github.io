1. From identification to estimation

Post 2 ended with the identification formula on the hazard scale:

S^a(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k} \left(1 - \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a)\right)\right].

This identity is structural: under the three conditions of post 2 §3, the unobservable counterfactual survival equals a function of the conditional hazard \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) and the covariate distribution F_{\mathbf L}. Both are population quantities. Neither is given to us in the data.

The plug-in idea is to replace each unknown ingredient with a sample estimate:

  • Replace \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) with a fitted conditional hazard \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) — modelled via pooled logistic regression on person-time data.
  • Replace \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L} with the empirical average over the observed sample, (1/n)\sum_i.

Combining the two substitutions:

\hat S^a_g(k) \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1 - \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L_i, a)\right),

the parametric g-formula on the hazard scale. The contrast follows directly:

\hat\Delta(k) \;=\; \hat S^1_g(k) - \hat S^0_g(k).

The rest of this post is operational. Section 2 specifies the conditional hazard model. Section 3 walks the three-step algorithm — fit the model, predict counterfactual hazards at every subject-interval, marginalize over the sample. Sections 4 and 5 handle the worked example and censoring. Section 6 closes.

2. The conditional hazard model: pooled logistic on person-time

The plug-in estimator of section 1 requires a model for the conditional hazard \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A). Building it needs the right data layout, the right target quantity, and the right regression tool.

Data layout. Post 1 §5 transformed subject-level data from wide format (one row per subject) to long (person-time) format (one row per at-risk subject-interval). Each long-format row carries the subject’s id, the interval j, the treatment A_i, baseline covariates \mathbf L_i, and the binary event indicator Y_{i,j} at the end of the interval. A subject contributes one row for each interval they are at risk, until they have the event or are censored.

The target is a conditional probability. The hazard at interval j is, by definition (post 1 §4):

\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A) \;=\; \mathbb{P}[Y_j = 1 \mid Y_{j-1} = 0,\ \bar C_{j-1} = 0,\ \mathbf L,\ A].

It is the chance of an event during interval j given covariates, treatment, and being at risk at the start of the interval — a conditional probability of an event.

Logistic regression models conditional probabilities. Given predictors X, logistic regression estimates

\mathbb{P}[Z = 1 \mid X] \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\beta^\top X)},

via the logit link \text{logit}\,\mathbb{P}[Z = 1 \mid X] = \beta^\top X. The fitted output is a probability in [0, 1].

Logistic regression on person-time is therefore a hazard model. Fit a logistic regression on the long-format dataset, with Y_{i,j} as the binary outcome and (j, A_i, \mathbf L_i) as predictors:

\text{logit}\,\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A) \;=\; \beta_0(j) + \beta_A\, A + \boldsymbol\beta_L^{\!\top} \mathbf L.

Each long-format row corresponds to a subject who was at risk during interval j; the outcome is the event indicator at the end of that interval; so the conditional probability the model is estimating is precisely the hazard. The model is fit by maximum likelihood, treating each at-risk row as an independent Bernoulli draw. The function \beta_0(j) — splines, polynomials, or interval indicators — gives the hazard flexibility over time.

Why “pooled”. Pooled-logistic regression1 refers to estimating all interval-specific hazards from a single model: the time index j enters as a predictor, not as a stratifier. Under short intervals, pooled-logistic and Cox proportional-hazards estimates align (D’Agostino et al. 1990).

Once fitted, the model produces \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L_i, a) for any (j, \mathbf L_i, a) — including counterfactual values of a a subject did not actually receive. That capability is what makes the next step — the g-formula algorithm — possible.

3. The g-formula algorithm: clone, predict, marginalize

The plug-in estimator from §1 is

\hat S^a_g(k) \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1 - \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L_i, a)\right).

§2 fitted the hazard model \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A). Plugging it in takes three steps.

1. Clone. For each subject i, take the baseline row at k = 0 — carrying \mathbf L_i — and replicate it across all intervals k = 1, \ldots, K_{\max}, setting A_i = a on every replicated row. Do this twice: once for a = 1, once for a = 0. The result is two cloned datasets, each of size n \cdot K_{\max}.

Why clone baseline rows, not the subject’s full set of observed at-risk rows?

In v1, \mathbf L is baseline-only — measured once, constant within subject. So the content of the baseline row and any at-risk row is identical for the model’s predictors. The only thing that changes between rows of the same subject is the interval index k. The key difference is which k values are produced.

  • Clone baseline: every subject gets K_{\max} prediction rows, k = 1, \ldots, K_{\max}. A subject censored at month 5 still gets predictions for j = 6, \ldots, 48.
  • Use observed at-risk rows: every subject gets only the rows they were actually observed at risk. A subject censored at month 5 gets predictions for j = 1, \ldots, 5 only.

Why this matters for the marginalization. The estimator has the same n subjects at every k. The marginalization is over the full sample’s \mathbf L distribution, fixed across k. That matches the identification target \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}[\ldots].

Under the at-risk-rows alternative, the sum at k=48 only includes subjects observed past month 48. At k=5, it includes everyone past month 5. The denominator (and the \mathbf L distribution being averaged over) changes with k — informative dropout bias baked into the estimator. The earlier observed events and censoring would silently filter who contributes to the marginal at each k, and that filter is not random.

Cloning baseline keeps the marginal target stable across k. It is an identification consistency requirement, not a coding convenience.

2. Predict. Use the fitted model from §2 to compute \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L_i, a) on every cloned row. The prediction is made for every interval j — including intervals past the subject’s observed exit time. Counterfactually, we are asking: “if subject i were assigned treatment a and had not been censored, what would their per-interval hazard be?” The cloned dataset gives \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L_i, a) for every (i, j, a).

Why does “set A_i = a and predict” give a counterfactual hazard?

The fitted model is

\hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A) \;\approx\; \mathbb{P}[Y_j = 1 \mid Y_{j-1}=0,\ \bar C_{j-1}=0,\ \mathbf L,\ A].

This is an observational conditional probability — fit from observed (j, \mathbf L_i, A_i, Y_{i,j}) rows. Nothing counterfactual yet.

When we clone-and-predict, we evaluate this observational conditional at A = a — including for subjects who actually received 1-a. The question is why this observational quantity is interpretable as the counterfactual hazard.

It isn’t, in general. It is interpretable that way only under exchangeability given \mathbf L:

\underbrace{\mathbb{P}[Y_j = 1 \mid Y_{j-1}=0,\ \bar C_{j-1}=0,\ \mathbf L,\ A=a]}_{\text{observational; what the model fits}} \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{P}[Y^a_j = 1 \mid Y^a_{j-1}=0,\ \mathbf L]}_{\text{counterfactual; what we want}}.

The two sides are equal because \mathbf L contains all confounders. Without that, the LHS averages over unmeasured U with the wrong weights — the observational distribution U \mid \mathbf L, A=a, not the counterfactual marginal U \mid \mathbf L. These differ when U is associated with both A and Y.

“Set A=a and predict” is not a counterfactual trick. It’s a literal evaluation of the fitted model at A=a. The counterfactual interpretation is bought by the assumption made before fitting. The clone-and-predict step is mechanical; the causal warrant lives in the assumption.

Corollary. The model must include all confounders in \mathbf L. Omitting one is not a “small error” the algorithm can absorb — it breaks the equality above, and the predicted hazard is no longer counterfactual at all.

3. Marginalize. For each subject and each k, compute the per-subject counterfactual survival via the product representation:

\hat S^a_i(k) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1 - \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L_i, a)\right).

Average across the sample to obtain the population estimate:

\hat S^a_g(k) \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat S^a_i(k).

The contrast follows:

\hat\Delta(k) \;=\; \hat S^1_g(k) - \hat S^0_g(k).

4. Worked example

We run the g-formula on the simulated observational DGP from post 2: 1000 patients, age and sex measured at baseline, drug assignment depending on both. The true treatment effect is known from the DGP, so estimates can be compared.

Three estimates of arm-specific survival are computed:

  • Naive arm-specific Kaplan-Meier. No covariate adjustment. Subjects stratified by observed A_i; the standard-KM curve from post 1 §3 computed separately within each arm.
  • G-formula. Pooled-logistic Y-hazard fit on the observational person-time data, then clone-predict-marginalize as in §3.
  • DGP truth. Arm-specific counterfactual survival computed analytically from the DGP — what \hat S^a_g should recover under correct specification and the identifiability conditions of post 2 §3.

The naive arm-specific KM is biased because the treated and untreated groups differ in age and sex. The crude curves estimate the observational \mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0 \mid A = a] — which post 2 §6 already showed is not the counterfactual S^a(k).

The g-formula curves recover the truth (up to sampling variation) because the pooled-logistic model adjusts for \mathbf L = (\text{age}, \text{sex}) — the only confounders in our DGP. The two identifying assumptions are correct here by construction: no unmeasured confounders (exchangeability given \mathbf L) and overlap in age and sex across arms (positivity).

R code for the worked example 
# Person-time data from the simulated DGP
pt_data <- to_person_time(
  data       = simulated_obs_data,
  id         = "id",
  time       = "obs_time",
  status     = "event",
  treatment  = "A",
  covariates = c("age", "sex"),
  cut_points = 48
)

# Naive arm-specific KM (no covariate adjustment)
km_a1 <- survival::survfit(
  Surv(obs_time, event) ~ 1,
  data = subset(simulated_obs_data, A == 1)
)
km_a0 <- survival::survfit(
  Surv(obs_time, event) ~ 1,
  data = subset(simulated_obs_data, A == 0)
)

# G-formula
gf_fit   <- causal_survival(
  pt_data  = pt_data,
  method   = "gformula",
  formulas = list(y = y_flag ~ k + A + age + sex)
)
gf_S_a1  <- gf_fit$surv_a1
gf_S_a0  <- gf_fit$surv_a0
gf_delta <- gf_S_a1 - gf_S_a0

5. Censoring under the g-formula

The g-formula’s Y-hazard model is fit on uncensored at-risk rows: subjects with \bar C_{j-1} = 0 in each interval j. The fitted hazard \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A) is therefore a conditional-on-being-uncensored hazard:

\hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A) \;\approx\; \mathbb{P}[Y_j = 1 \mid Y_{j-1} = 0,\ \bar C_{j-1} = 0,\ \mathbf L,\ A].

When this is plugged into the product representation, the resulting \hat S^a_g(k) accumulates per-interval survivals without subtracting anything for censoring. The estimator therefore targets the no-censoring counterfactual — survival under treatment a if no patient had been censored.

Under what condition is this also the target S^a(k)? Conditional exchangeability of censoring given (\mathbf L, A):

C_j \perp\!\!\!\perp Y^a_j \mid \mathbf L,\ A,\ \bar C_{j-1} = 0,\ Y_{j-1} = 0.

If censoring at j is independent of the counterfactual outcome given baseline covariates, treatment, and the past, then the uncensored hazard equals the no-censoring counterfactual hazard. The product representation gives S^a(k).

If the assumption fails — for instance, sicker patients dropping out at higher rates even after conditioning on \mathbf L and A — the fitted hazard is biased for the no-censoring counterfactual, and so is \hat S^a_g(k).3

For the simulated DGP of §4, censoring is independent of the counterfactual outcome by construction. The assumption is satisfied; the g-formula recovers the truth. In real data, the assumption must be argued from the structure of the study and the measured covariates — the same substantive work the treatment identifiability conditions require (post 2 §3).

6. Closing

The parametric g-formula is the plug-in estimator of the identification formula of post 2 §4. It models the outcome process — specifically, the conditional hazard \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, A) — and combines the fitted model with the empirical covariate distribution to estimate the counterfactual survival S^a(k) and the risk-difference contrast \Delta(k).

For the estimator to be unbiased, two things must hold:

  1. The identifiability conditions of post 2 §3 — exchangeability, positivity, consistency — for the treatment process, plus conditional exchangeability of censoring (§5).
  2. Correct specification of the conditional hazard model.

Both are assumptions about the world (1) and about the analyst (2), respectively. The identifiability conditions are unverifiable from the data alone; the model specification can be partially checked.

Bridge to post 4. The g-formula models the outcome. The companion method — IPW — models the treatment assignment instead. The two estimators target the same identification formula but rely on the correctness of different models. Post 4 develops IPW, and includes (as a collapsible) the comparison of why g-formula needs one model and IPW needs two.
