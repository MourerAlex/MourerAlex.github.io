1. From a trial to observational data

The same drugs, the same outcome, the same 1000 patients — but now the data come from an observational cohort, not a randomized trial. Each patient at elevated heart-failure risk has been prescribed weekly Drug A or weekly Drug B by their physician. The prescription was not made by coin flip. It depended on the patient: age, sex, comorbidity profile, prior medications, and how each physician interprets clinical guidelines.

The estimand we want is unchanged — the survival contrast between Drug A and Drug B. The route to it is not. An arm-specific Kaplan-Meier estimate computed on this dataset will produce two curves, just as it did in the trial. But the curves no longer estimate the causal quantity. The treated and untreated patients differ in ways that affect heart-failure risk independently of which drug they took, as for instance one group may be older on average.

This is confounding. In a DAG, baseline covariates \mathbf L point both into the treatment A (physicians prescribed by age and sex) and into the outcome Y_k (age and sex affect heart-failure risk regardless of drug):

The rest of this post answers two questions. First, what is the causal quantity we wanted in the first place, stated precisely? Second, what conditions on the data — and on the analysis — are required to recover it?

2. The counterfactual survival difference

Imagine a hypothetical world in which the patients from the observational cohort could each be assessed under both treatments. Each patient receives Drug A; we observe their time to heart failure. We then go back in time and rerun the assessment, this time giving every patient Drug B. Every patient contributes two outcomes. The two populations are made of identical individuals, and any difference between them is attributable to the drug.

These hypothetical outcomes are called counterfactual: one is factual (the one actually observed), the other counter to the fact. We denote them Y^a_k — the event indicator at interval k under counterfactual treatment a.

For each individual with observed treatment A = a, the observed outcome equals the counterfactual under their treatment: Y_k = Y^A_k. This equality is consistency — the observed outcome is consistent with the intervention received.

Since the hypothetical groups are made of identical individuals, they are exchangeable: one-to-one comparable. In that world, each individual has a measurable causal effect — the difference between their two outcomes — and averaging over the population gives the average causal effect, the difference in counterfactual survival curves at k:

\Delta(k) \;=\; S^1(k) - S^0(k), \quad \text{where} \quad S^a(k) \;=\; \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0].

A Kaplan-Meier difference on the stacked dataset of factual and counterfactual outcomes would estimate \Delta(k) without bias. But the stacked dataset is not observable: each patient contributes only the factual outcome.

Under the perfectly conducted RCT of post 1, randomization makes the treatment groups exchangeable in expectation, and the treatment assignment is well-defined so consistency holds. The average causal effect \Delta(k) is therefore identifiable from the observed data, and the difference of arm-specific KM curves estimates it without bias.

In observational data, neither exchangeability nor consistency follows automatically. The next section states the conditions required for identifiability when randomization is absent.

3. Identifiability: when do observational data identify \Delta(k)?

Section 2 stated consistency and exchangeability in the hypothetical world. To recover \Delta(k) from observational data, both conditions must hold in a stronger form, and one additional condition — positivity — joins them.

  • Consistency. The treatment A must correspond to a well-defined intervention. If Drug A in the registry covers several doses and schedules, Y^a is not a single counterfactual outcome and the contrast against Drug B is ambiguous. Comparing “aspirin for 1 year” against “aspirin for 2 years” when the two groups received very different daily doses from the start violates consistency — the intervention is not a single thing.

  • Exchangeability given \mathbf L. The no-unmeasured-confounders assumption: Y^a_k \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid \mathbf L. After conditioning on the baseline covariates \mathbf L, the treated and untreated patients are comparable. Every common cause of treatment and outcome must be in \mathbf L. An unmeasured prognostic factor that also influences prescription would break this condition.

  • Positivity given \mathbf L. Every covariate profile must have a non-zero probability of receiving each treatment: 0 < g(a \mid \mathbf L) < 1. Example: if Drug B is never prescribed to patients under 50 because of contraindications, g(0 \mid \text{age} < 50) = 0 and S^0(k) is not identifiable for that subgroup.

Under the perfectly conducted RCT of post 1, all three held without statistical work: randomization gave exchangeability unconditionally, the trial protocol gave consistency, and the two-arm design guaranteed positivity.

In observational data, none of these is automatic. Each must be argued from the study structure and the measured covariates.1

4. The identification formula

Under the three conditions of Section 3, the counterfactual survival function S^a(k) equals a standardized version of the observable conditional survival:

S^a(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\,\mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0 \mid A = a,\ \mathbf L]\,\right].

Derivation: from S^a(k) to the identification formula

Start with what we want: S^a(k) = \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0].

  1. Algebra. \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}[\mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0 \mid \mathbf L]] — law of total expectation. No assumption.

  2. Exchangeability. \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0 \mid \mathbf L] = \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0 \mid A=a, \mathbf L] — because Y^a_k \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid \mathbf L. Within strata of \mathbf L, the counterfactual is the same regardless of actual treatment.

  3. Consistency. \mathbb{P}[Y^a_k = 0 \mid A=a, \mathbf L] = \mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0 \mid A=a, \mathbf L] — for subjects with A=a, the observed Y_k is Y^a_k.

Combining: S^a(k) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}[\mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0 \mid A=a, \mathbf L]]. Every quantity on the right is observable.

Positivity is implicit throughout: \mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0 \mid A=a, \mathbf L = l] must be well-defined for every l in the support, requiring \mathbb{P}[A=a \mid \mathbf L = l] > 0.

The hazard-scale form follows by factoring the inner conditional via the product representation of post 1 §4:

\mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0 \mid A=a, \mathbf L] \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1 - \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a)\right).

The inner conditional probability factorises into a product of one-step survivals — the same product representation of post 1, now conditional on covariates and treatment:

\mathbb{P}[Y_k = 0 \mid A = a,\ \mathbf L] \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{k} \left(1 - \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a)\right),

where \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) = \mathbb{P}[Y_j = 1 \mid Y_{j-1} = 0, \bar C_{j-1} = 0, \mathbf L, A = a] is the conditional hazard at interval j. Combining the two:

S^a(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k} \left(1 - \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a)\right)\right].

Read verbally: for each value of \mathbf L, compute the conditional survival curve under treatment a — the product of one-step survivals at each interval — then average over the covariate distribution in the target population. The two-arm contrast follows:

\Delta(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k} \left(1 - \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, 1)\right)\right] - \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf L}\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k} \left(1 - \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, 0)\right)\right].

This is the identification formula on the hazard scale. Under the conditions of Section 3, the unobservable counterfactual survival is exactly equal to a quantity constructed from the observable conditional hazards and the observable covariate distribution.

In post 1, \lambda^Y_j had no \mathbf L argument: the trial was unconfounded, and a single marginal hazard sufficed. In observational data, the hazard depends on \mathbf L, and the formula above requires the full conditional surface \{\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) : j, \mathbf L, a\}. The next section asks what it would take to estimate this surface from a sample of 1000 patients.

5. Why we need a model

The identification formula of Section 4 requires the conditional hazard \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) as a function of \mathbf L and a. Without a model, the natural estimator is nonparametric: within each combination of covariate values and treatment, compute the empirical hazard from the at-risk subjects.

Count the cells. Suppose \mathbf L comprises age (split into deciles, 8 levels) and sex (2 levels), giving 16 covariate cells. Cross with 2 treatments: 32 cells. With 1000 patients in total, that’s about 31 patients per cell on average — but the distribution is uneven (treatment is not random across age × sex), and we need not just patients but events in each cell to estimate a hazard. Many cells will hold few events; some will hold none.

Add a third covariate with modest cardinality, and the cells multiply quickly. Add a continuous covariate, and the cell count is technically infinite. This is the curse of dimensionality on the covariate × treatment grid.

A model trades cell-by-cell nonparametric estimation for a parametric structure that pools information across cells. Pooled-logistic regression on the person-time data layout of post 1 (Section 5) is the standard choice — each at-risk person-interval row contributes one observation, with Y_{i,k} as the binary outcome and (j, \mathbf L, a) as predictors. Once the model is fit, \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) is available for every (j, \mathbf L, a), including cells with no observed subjects. The parametric form is the assumption that makes this possible.

Section 6 previews two ways the identification formula is combined with such a hazard model: IPW and the g-formula.

6. Two estimation routes: IPW and the g-formula

Section 4 specifies the causal estimand; it does not specify the estimator to produce an estimate. Two routes are standard.

Plug-in (g-formula). Fit a model for the conditional hazard \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) on the person-time data of post 1. Use it to predict, for each subject i and each interval j, the conditional one-step survival 1 - \hat\lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L_i, a) — once under a = 1, once under a = 0. Average the product across the sample’s covariate distribution to obtain \hat S^a(k), and difference the two arms to obtain \hat\Delta(k). Post 3 develops it.

Reweighting (IPW). Fit a model for the propensity score g(A \mid \mathbf L) — the probability of receiving each treatment given baseline covariates — and assign each subject a weight inversely proportional to their probability of having received the treatment they actually received. The weighted sample reproduces a pseudo-population in which A and \mathbf L are independent: confounding is broken by construction. Arm-specific weighted Kaplan-Meier on the pseudo-population estimates S^a(k), and a difference estimates \Delta(k). Post 4 develops it.

The two routes share the same target — the identification formula of Section 4 — but model different objects: the outcome process (g-formula) or the treatment assignment (IPW). Figure 6.1 makes the distinction visible on the DAG.

7. Closing

In a perfectly conducted RCT, the question of what would happen if every patient received Drug A versus Drug B is answered directly: randomization makes the arm-specific Kaplan-Meier curves estimates of the causal contrast, with no further assumptions to defend. Yet randomized trials are often unethical, impractical, or untimely. Treatments cannot always be randomly assigned — ethics committees would not approve every intervention, study patients may not comply, blinding may be impossible, and the disease may run faster than the trial can recruit. Frequently, conducting an observational study is the least bad option. The data are real-world; the question of causal effect remains the same.

Observational data, on its own, gives us association, not causation. The arm-specific \hat S_{\text{KM}}(k \mid A=a) on observational data answers “what is the risk in those who received Drug a?”, not “what would the risk be if everyone had received Drug a?”. The two answers can differ enormously, and when they do, association masquerading as causation can drive bad clinical decisions. Hernán and Robins give the canonical example: if aspirin’s causal risk ratio for cardiovascular death is 0.5 but its associational risk ratio is 1.5 — because aspirin is preferentially prescribed to high-risk patients — a physician who acts on the association will withhold a protective drug.

The three identifiability conditions of Section 3 are exactly the bridge from association to causation. Without them, the observational S(k \mid A=a) is a description of the data; under them, it identifies the counterfactual S^a(k). The cost is that the conditions are unverifiable and must be argued from substantive knowledge of the data-generating process and the measured covariates.

Post 3 develops the g-formula: estimate the conditional hazard \lambda^Y_j(\mathbf L, a) by pooled logistic regression, plug into the identification formula, average across covariates, contrast arms.

Post 4 develops IPW: estimate the propensity g(A \mid \mathbf L), build subject-level weights, fit arm-specific weighted Kaplan-Meier on the pseudo-population, contrast arms.
